What is Gradient Descent?

Machine Learning · optimization · training · learning rate

1Definition

Gradient descent is an iterative optimization algorithm that finds parameter values minimizing a loss function. At each step it computes the gradient — the direction of steepest increase of the loss — and moves the parameters a small amount in the opposite direction. The size of that move is controlled by the learning rate. Repeated many times, the parameters slide "downhill" on the loss surface toward a minimum. It is the engine that trains nearly every machine-learning model, from linear regression to trillion-parameter neural networks (via its variants SGD, Momentum, and Adam).

2Analogy

You're standing on a foggy mountainside trying to reach the valley floor. You can't see the valley — you can only feel the slope under your feet (the gradient). So you repeatedly take a step in the steepest downhill direction. Your stride length is the learning rate: tiny steps get you there safely but slowly; huge leaps might overshoot the valley and land you on the opposite slope — or bounce you right out of the mountains (divergence). And if the terrain has several valleys, you might settle into a shallow one (a local minimum) without ever knowing a deeper one exists nearby.

4History → Current → Future

1847
Augustin-Louis Cauchy proposes the method of steepest descent for solving systems of equations — gradient descent predates computers by a century.
1951
Robbins & Monro formalize stochastic approximation — the theory behind stochastic gradient descent (SGD), which uses noisy gradient estimates from data samples.
1986
Backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams) gives an efficient way to compute gradients through multi-layer networks, making gradient descent practical for deep learning.
2014
Adam combines momentum with per-parameter adaptive learning rates and becomes the default optimizer for deep networks.
Current
Every frontier LLM is trained with SGD-family optimizers (AdamW dominant) on thousands of GPUs, with learning-rate schedules (warmup + cosine decay), gradient clipping, and mixed precision. Newer optimizers (Lion, Shampoo/SOAP, Muon) compete on wall-clock efficiency.
Future
Research pushes toward second-order and curvature-aware methods made cheap enough for scale, learned optimizers, and training schemes that cut the enormous energy cost of descent at trillion-parameter scale.

5Example

Minimize f(x) = x² starting at x = 4 with learning rate η = 0.1. The gradient is f′(x) = 2x, and the update rule is x ← x − η·f′(x) = x − 0.2x = 0.8x:

  • Step 0: x = 4.000, f(x) = 16.000
  • Step 1: x = 4 − 0.1·8 = 3.200, f(x) = 10.240
  • Step 2: x = 3.2 − 0.1·6.4 = 2.560, f(x) = 6.554
  • Step 3: x = 2.048, f(x) = 4.194 … each step shrinks x by 20%
  • Step 20: x ≈ 0.046 — essentially at the minimum x = 0.

With η = 1.1 instead, the multiplier becomes −1.2: x jumps to −4.8, then +5.76 … growing every step. Same algorithm, wrong learning rate → divergence.

6Use cases

  • Training neural networks: LLMs, image models, and recommenders all fit billions of weights by descending a loss (cross-entropy, MSE) via backpropagated gradients.
  • Classical ML: logistic/linear regression and SVMs are routinely fit with (stochastic) gradient descent when datasets are too big for closed-form solutions.
  • Fine-tuning & RLHF: adapting a pre-trained model to your data is more gradient descent, just starting from good weights.
  • Physics & engineering: minimizing energy functions, calibrating simulations, portfolio optimization in finance.
  • Adversarial examples: gradient ascent on the input (not the weights) crafts inputs that fool a model — the same math, reversed.

7Math & Python

θt+1 = θt − η ∇θL(θt)

θ = parameters, η = learning rate, ∇θL = gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters. For convex L with a suitable η, this provably converges; for deep networks it finds good (not necessarily global) minima remarkably well.

# Gradient descent from scratch — fitting y = w*x + b by minimizing MSE
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
X = rng.uniform(0, 10, 100)
y = 3.0 * X + 7.0 + rng.normal(0, 1, 100)   # true w=3, b=7, plus noise

w, b, lr = 0.0, 0.0, 0.01

for step in range(2000):
    y_hat = w * X + b
    error = y_hat - y
    loss = (error ** 2).mean()               # MSE loss
    grad_w = 2 * (error * X).mean()          # dL/dw
    grad_b = 2 * error.mean()                # dL/db
    w -= lr * grad_w                         # the descent step
    b -= lr * grad_b
    if step % 500 == 0:
        print(f"step {step:4d}  loss={loss:8.3f}  w={w:.3f}  b={b:.3f}")

print(f"final: w={w:.3f} (true 3.0), b={b:.3f} (true 7.0)")
