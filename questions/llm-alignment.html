What is alignment in LLMs, and what are the different types?

LLMs · alignment · RLHF · DPO · safety

1Definition

Alignment is the problem of making an AI system actually pursue what its designers and users intend, rather than whatever proxy objective it was literally trained on. A pretrained LLM optimizes exactly one thing — predict the next token in web text — which makes it a fluent imitator, not a helpful assistant. It will happily continue a conversation with more questions instead of answering, imitate confident nonsense, or reproduce harmful content, because all of that is faithful next-token prediction. Alignment is the set of techniques that closes the gap between "predicts plausible text" and "does what you actually wanted, safely and honestly."

The usual target is summarized as the HHH criteria — Helpful (does the task), Honest (doesn't fabricate, expresses calibrated uncertainty), and Harmless (declines to cause serious harm). These genuinely trade off against each other: a model that refuses everything is perfectly harmless and useless, which is why over-refusal is treated as an alignment failure too, not a safety win.

2Analogy

Think of hiring a brilliant new employee who has read the entire internet but has never held a job. They know an enormous amount, but on day one they don't know your norms: asked "how do I fix this?" they might recite a forum thread, confidently invent a plausible answer, or repeat something they read that would get the company sued. Nothing is wrong with their knowledge — they simply have no idea what behavior is wanted.

So you train them. First you show worked examples of good responses — that's supervised fine-tuning, like shadowing a senior colleague. Then you give feedback: "that answer was better than this one" — that's RLHF/DPO, learning from preferences rather than from a rulebook. And you hand them an employee handbook of principles to self-check against — that's Constitutional AI. The classic failure mode is equally familiar: an employee optimizing the metric instead of the job — closing tickets fast by marking them resolved. That's reward hacking, and it's the central difficulty of alignment.

4History → Current → Future

1960
Norbert Wiener articulates the core worry decades early: "if we use, to achieve our purposes, a mechanical agency… we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire."
2017
Christiano et al. publish Deep RL from Human Preferences — training a reward model from pairwise human comparisons instead of hand-written reward functions. This is the technical seed of RLHF.
2022
InstructGPT (Ouyang et al.) shows a 1.3B aligned model beating the 175B base GPT-3 on human preference — alignment, not just scale, is what makes models useful. Anthropic formalizes the HHH framing and publishes Constitutional AI (RLAIF), replacing much human harm-labeling with AI self-critique against written principles. ChatGPT launches, making RLHF the industry default.
2023–24
DPO (Rafailov et al.) proves you can optimize the same RLHF objective in closed form with a simple classification loss — no separate reward model, no PPO loop — and it becomes the default for open-source alignment. Variants proliferate (IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO); scalable-oversight work (debate, weak-to-strong generalization) targets tasks humans can't directly grade.
Current
Post-training is a full pipeline: SFT → preference optimization (DPO/PPO/GRPO) → RLAIF/constitutional passes → red-teaming → evals. Attention has shifted to reasoning-model alignment (aligning chains of thought, not just final answers), deliberative alignment, interpretability-informed methods, and measuring over-refusal as carefully as harm.
Future
The open problems are scalable oversight (supervising systems more capable than their supervisors), inner alignment (guaranteeing the learned objective — not just observed behavior — is right), robustness to jailbreaks, and pluralistic alignment: whose values, chosen how, given that "aligned" is not one fixed target.

5Example

Prompt: "Explain how vaccines work."

  • Base (pretrained) model: "Explain how antibiotics work. Explain how the immune system works. Explain how…" — it continues the pattern of a list of questions, because that's what such text looks like on the web. Not disobedient, just unaligned.
  • After SFT: "Vaccines work by training your immune system…" — it now answers, because it imitated thousands of demonstration answers. But quality is capped by the demonstrations, and it may still fabricate confidently.
  • After RLHF/DPO: a well-structured explanation that flags uncertainty and adds appropriate caveats — because human raters consistently preferred responses with those properties, and the model was optimized toward that preference signal.

Now the alignment tax, made concrete. Ask "What household chemicals shouldn't I mix?" — a legitimate safety question. An over-harmlessness-tuned model refuses ("I can't help with creating dangerous substances"), which is harmless but useless and arguably less safe than answering. Meanwhile a purely helpfulness-tuned model would cheerfully answer a genuinely dangerous version of the same question. The target is the narrow band between the two — which is exactly why alignment is hard to specify, not just hard to optimize.

6Types of alignment — and where each is used

By technique (how you align):

SFT / Instruction tuning
Supervised imitation of demonstration answers. Cheap, stable, the mandatory first step — but capped by demonstrator quality and can't express "A is better than B."
RLHF (PPO)
Train a reward model on human pairwise preferences, then optimize the policy against it with RL + a KL penalty to stay near the SFT model. Powerful; complex, unstable, and vulnerable to reward hacking.
DPO & friends
Skip the reward model — derive a closed-form classification loss over preference pairs that optimizes the same objective. Simpler and cheaper; now the open-source default (IPO/KTO/ORPO/SimPO are variants).
RLAIF / Constitutional AI
Replace most human harm labels with an AI critiquing and revising its own outputs against an explicit written constitution. Scales cheaply and makes the values auditable in plain text.
Inference-time alignment
System prompts, guardrail/classifier models, best-of-n sampling against a reward model, deliberative self-checks. No retraining, instantly updatable — but shallower and easier to jailbreak.

By what's being aligned (conceptual types — the framing interviewers probe):

Outer alignment
Is the specified objective the right one? Failure = a reward model that rewards long, confident-sounding answers, so the model learns sycophancy and verbosity.
Inner alignment
Given a correct objective, does the model actually internalize it — or just learn something correlated that holds on the training distribution and breaks off it? (a.k.a. goal misgeneralization.)
Intent vs. value alignment
Following what the user literally asked (intent) versus what they'd endorse on reflection, plus society's broader values. These conflict: the literal request isn't always the right thing to do.
Personal / pluralistic
Whose preferences? RLHF averages over a rater pool, embedding those raters' biases. Steerable and culturally pluralistic alignment is active research.

7Math & Python

Preferences are modeled with Bradley-Terry — the same formula behind Elo. Given a reward model r, the probability a human prefers response y₁ over y₂ for prompt x:

P(y1 ≻ y2 | x) = σ( r(x, y1) − r(x, y2) )

RLHF then maximizes that reward while a KL penalty keeps the policy from drifting away from the SFT model (drift is what reward hacking looks like):

maxπ 𝔼x, y∼π [ r(x, y) ] − β · KL( π(y|x) ‖ πref(y|x) )

DPO's insight: that constrained objective has a closed-form optimum, so the reward model can be eliminated entirely and the whole thing becomes one classification loss over preference pairs (yw = preferred/"win", yl = rejected/"lose"):

ℒDPO = − 𝔼 [ log σ( β log π(yw|x)/πref(yw|x) − β log π(yl|x)/πref(yl|x) ) ]

β controls how tightly the policy is tethered to the reference model: small β allows big behavior changes (and more reward hacking), large β keeps it conservative.

import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

# --- Bradley-Terry: how a reward model turns scores into preference probability
r_win, r_lose = 2.4, 1.1
print("P(prefer the better response) =", round(sigmoid(r_win - r_lose), 3))   # 0.786

# --- The DPO loss, from scratch -------------------------------------------
# Inputs are SUMMED log-probs of each full response under the policy and the
# frozen reference (SFT) model.
def dpo_loss(lp_pol_w, lp_pol_l, lp_ref_w, lp_ref_l, beta=0.1):
    """Implicit reward = beta * (policy logprob - reference logprob)."""
    margin_w = beta * (lp_pol_w - lp_ref_w)      # implicit reward, preferred
    margin_l = beta * (lp_pol_l - lp_ref_l)      # implicit reward, rejected
    return -np.log(sigmoid(margin_w - margin_l))

ref_w, ref_l = -12.0, -12.0                       # SFT model: no preference

# Policy has NOT yet learned the preference -> high loss
print("untrained :", round(dpo_loss(-12.0, -12.0, ref_w, ref_l), 4))   # 0.6931 = log 2

# Policy raised P(preferred) and lowered P(rejected) -> loss drops
print("aligned   :", round(dpo_loss(-8.0, -20.0, ref_w, ref_l), 4))

# --- Why the KL term matters: reward hacking ------------------------------
# A policy that drifts far from the reference can score high on a flawed
# reward model while degenerating (e.g. rambling, sycophancy).
for kl in [0.0, 2.0, 10.0, 40.0]:
    proxy_reward = 1.5 * np.log1p(kl)     # flawed RM keeps "improving"
    true_quality = 1.5 * np.log1p(kl) - 0.25 * kl   # actual quality turns over
    print(f"KL={kl:5.1f}  proxy reward={proxy_reward:6.2f}   true quality={true_quality:6.2f}")
