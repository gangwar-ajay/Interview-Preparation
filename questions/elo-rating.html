What is the Elo Rating System, and how is it used to compare LLMs?

LLMs · evaluation · Chatbot Arena · pairwise comparison

1Definition

The Elo rating system is a method for ranking competitors from a stream of pairwise, win/loss/draw outcomes — invented by Arpad Elo for chess. Every competitor starts with a rating (often 1000 or 1500). Before each match, the rating difference predicts a win probability via a logistic curve. After the match, both ratings move by an amount proportional to the surprise: beating a much stronger opponent gains you a lot; beating a much weaker one barely moves the needle. Ratings self-correct over many matches into a single number that predicts future head-to-head outcomes.

For LLMs, this is exactly how Chatbot Arena (LMSYS/LMArena) ranks models: real users send a prompt, get anonymous side-by-side responses from two random models, and vote for the better one — no rubric, no benchmark questions, just human preference. Each vote is one Elo "match." With enough votes across enough model pairs, Elo converges to a single leaderboard number per model, mirroring how chess federations rank millions of players from disconnected local games.

2Analogy

Think of a ladder tournament at a chess club that never plays a full round-robin — players just challenge whoever's around. If the club's #1 player beats a beginner, almost nothing changes: everyone already expected that. But if the beginner pulls off an upset, they jump several rungs at once, because that result was informative — it revealed the beginner is stronger than the ladder thought. Elo is the bookkeeping that makes this rigorous: your rating moves by K × (what actually happened − what was expected). Chatbot Arena is the same ladder, except the "matches" are anonymous users picking their favorite of two chatbot replies, thousands of times a day, across models that never play a clean one-on-one round-robin either.

4History → Current → Future

1960
Physicist Arpad Elo designs the system to replace the US Chess Federation's ad-hoc ranking method with one grounded in a logistic performance model.
1970
FIDE (the World Chess Federation) adopts Elo as the official international rating system — it becomes the template copied by nearly every competitive-ranking domain since.
2010s
Glicko, Glicko-2, and TrueSkill (Microsoft, for Xbox Live matchmaking) extend Elo with rating uncertainty and multiplayer support — precursors to the statistically richer methods used today.
2023
UC Berkeley's LMSYS launches Chatbot Arena: anonymous, randomized side-by-side LLM battles judged by real users, ranked with Elo (later Bradley-Terry, a closely related maximum-likelihood model) — it becomes the de facto "human preference" leaderboard for LLMs, cited by nearly every major lab.
Current
Arena-style leaderboards report Bradley-Terry ratings with bootstrapped confidence intervals rather than raw sequential Elo, precisely because match order shouldn't matter for a leaderboard (see the math below); category-specific boards (coding, math, hard prompts, multi-turn) have appeared alongside the overall ranking; "style control" adjustments try to separate genuine quality from response length/formatting bias.
Future
More robust preference aggregation (removing voter and prompt-selection bias), automated LLM-judge battles to scale beyond human raters, and tighter integration between arena-style human preference and benchmark accuracy as complementary — not competing — signals.

5Example

Model A is rated 1200, Model B is rated 1000, K = 32.

  • Expected score for A: EA = 1 / (1 + 10(1000−1200)/400) = 1 / (1 + 10−0.5) ≈ 0.76 (A is favored to win about 76% of the time).
  • Upset — B wins: A's actual score SA = 0. New rating: 1200 + 32×(0 − 0.76) = 1200 − 24.3 = 1175.7. B correspondingly rises to 1024.3. One upset moves both ratings by ~24 points.
  • As expected — A wins: SA = 1. New rating: 1200 + 32×(1 − 0.76) = 1200 + 7.7 = 1207.7. Barely moves — the outcome carried little new information.

The asymmetry is the entire point: Elo updates are largest exactly when the result was least expected, which is when it's most informative about the true ranking.

6Use cases

  • Chatbot Arena / LMArena: the primary human-preference leaderboard for LLMs, driving real adoption decisions and press coverage of new model releases.
  • RLHF reward-model evaluation: comparing two candidate policies via pairwise human preference, the same math used to train the reward models behind RLHF.
  • A/B testing prompts or system messages: treating two prompt variants as "players" and letting real usage or an LLM judge vote determines which wins.
  • Recommender & ranking systems: ranking items (not just models) from pairwise click/preference signals.
  • Its home turf: chess, Go, esports (many games use Elo or Elo-derived systems like Glicko-2 and TrueSkill for matchmaking).
  • Red-teaming & jailbreak comparison: ranking attack/defense prompt variants by how often they "win" against a target model.

7Math & Python

Expected score of player A against player B, from the rating gap:

EA = 1 / (1 + 10(RB − RA)/400)

Update after the match, where SA ∈ {1, 0.5, 0} for win/draw/loss and K controls how much a single match can move the rating:

RA′ = RA + K · (SA − EA)

Note EA + EB = 1 and the rating change is always equal and opposite (RA gains exactly what RB loses) — Elo is a zero-sum redistribution of rating points. The 400 is a scaling constant chosen so "+400 rating" corresponds to a 10:1 expected odds; it's arbitrary but fixed by convention. Modern LLM arenas actually fit a Bradley-Terry model — the same win-probability formula in base e instead of sequential updates — via maximum likelihood over all recorded battles at once, because plain sequential Elo is order-dependent (processing the same matches in a different order gives slightly different final ratings) while Bradley-Terry gives one order-independent answer plus proper confidence intervals.

import numpy as np

def expected_score(r_a, r_b):
    return 1 / (1 + 10 ** ((r_b - r_a) / 400))

def update_elo(r_a, r_b, score_a, k=32):
    """score_a: 1 = A won, 0.5 = draw, 0 = A lost."""
    e_a = expected_score(r_a, r_b)
    r_a_new = r_a + k * (score_a - e_a)
    r_b_new = r_b + k * ((1 - score_a) - (1 - e_a))   # zero-sum: exact mirror
    return r_a_new, r_b_new

# The worked example above
r_a, r_b = 1200, 1000
print("E_A =", round(expected_score(r_a, r_b), 3))         # 0.76
print("upset (B wins):", update_elo(r_a, r_b, score_a=0))   # (1175.7, 1024.3)
print("as expected (A wins):", update_elo(r_a, r_b, score_a=1))  # (1207.7, 992.3)

# Simulate a mini "Chatbot Arena": 5 models, random pairings, sequential Elo
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
true_strength = {"A": 1300, "B": 1200, "C": 1100, "D": 1000, "E": 900}
ratings = {m: 1000.0 for m in true_strength}   # everyone starts equal

for _ in range(2000):
    m1, m2 = rng.choice(list(true_strength), size=2, replace=False)
    # "true" win probability comes from hidden strength; the vote is a coin flip on it
    p1 = expected_score(true_strength[m1], true_strength[m2])
    score1 = 1.0 if rng.random() < p1 else 0.0
    ratings[m1], ratings[m2] = update_elo(ratings[m1], ratings[m2], score1, k=16)

for m in sorted(ratings, key=ratings.get, reverse=True):
    print(f"{m}: {ratings[m]:7.1f}   (true strength rank order preserved: "
          f"{sorted(true_strength, key=true_strength.get, reverse=True)})")
